Howzit, Grüß Gott! A Saffa's Guide to Surviving and Thriving in Vienna

By Brendon James Kirk 2025 6 min read

It's a move that raises eyebrows. You're moving from where? To where? Swapping the year-round humidity and endless warm coastline of Durban for the grand, imperial, and distinctly seasonal city of Vienna is a study in contrasts. It's trading a laid-back, "just now" culture for one of meticulous planning, a braai for a Würstelstand, and the sound of Hadidas for the chiming of cathedral bells.

This isn't just a change of address; it's a recalibration of life itself. Here's a look at my journey from South Africa to Austria, navigating the cultural shifts, the language labyrinth, and the eye-opening realities of life in a "first-world" European capital.

The Culture Shock is Real (and Wonderful)

Durban has a rhythm. It's the easy-going chat with the cashier, the friendly "howzit" from a stranger, the relaxed attitude towards time that drives outsiders crazy but feels like home to us. Life is lived outdoors, plans are flexible, and there's a certain warmth that has nothing to do with the weather.

Vienna, on the other hand, runs on a different operating system.

  • Punctuality and Planning: The Viennese value punctuality. If a meeting is at 10:00, you are there at 9:55. Spontaneity takes a backseat to the holy Termin (appointment). My Durban-ingrained habit of being "fashionably late" was the first thing I had to unlearn.
  • The Famous Viennese "Grant": People will tell you the Viennese are grumpy (grantig). I've found it's less grumpiness and more a formal directness. The bubbly, surface-level friendliness of SA is replaced by a reserved respect. You might not get a life story from the baker, but you'll get an efficient, professional service. Once you break through that initial formal barrier, however, the warmth is genuine.
  • Sunday is Sacred: Remember frantic Sunday mall runs? Forget it. In Austria, Sunday is Sonntagsruhe (Sunday rest). Almost every shop is closed. This was a massive shock at first, but now I've come to appreciate it. It forces a slower pace, encouraging time in nature, visiting a museum, or enjoying coffee and cake in a classic Kaffeehaus.

"Bitte?" My Epic Battle with the German Language

In South Africa, with its 11 official languages, we're used to linguistic diversity. But the dominance of English means you can get by almost anywhere.

Not so in Vienna. While many younger people speak excellent English, daily life—from navigating bureaucracy to ordering a specific type of bread (Semmel, not Brötchen!)—is in German. And not just any German, but Austrian German, with its own beautiful and baffling vocabulary.

Learning the language has been the single most important key to unlocking this city. It started with Duolingo and polite panic, graduating to formal classes where I wrestled with the grammatical cases of der, die, das. There were embarrassing moments, like confidently asking for something nonsensical, but also moments of pure triumph: the first time I understood a joke, successfully argued with a utility company, or just had a simple, flowing conversation with a neighbour.

If you're moving here, my single biggest piece of advice is this: Start learning German yesterday. It transforms you from a tourist into a resident.

A Different World: Safety, Systems, and Sourdough

The term "first world" can feel loaded, but the differences in daily infrastructure and social systems are undeniable. It's the little things that constantly leave you in awe.

  • Public Transport: Coming from a car-centric city like Durban, the Wiener Linien (Vienna's public transport network) is a miracle. Trams, buses, and subways that are clean, safe, and run with mind-boggling punctuality. The ability to cross the entire city for a few Euros at any time of day or night, without a second thought for my safety, felt like a superpower.
  • Walking After Dark: This is perhaps the most profound change. The simple act of walking home from a restaurant after 10 PM, phone in hand, without a knot of anxiety in my stomach, is a freedom I will never take for granted. The high walls, electric fences, and security patrols of many South African suburbs are non-existent here.
  • The Paper Chase: Austria loves its bureaucracy. Registering your address (Meldezettel), getting your residence permit, and dealing with officialdom is a masterclass in paperwork. Everything is done by the book. It can be frustrating, but there's a flip side: the system, for the most part, works.
  • Recycling is a Religion: Forget one bin for everything. Here, you become an expert in separating paper, glass (white and coloured), metal, plastic, and organic waste. It's a small change in habit that reflects a larger, collective sense of responsibility.

What I Miss, What I've Gained

Do I miss South Africa? Every single day. I miss the smell of a braai and the taste of proper biltong. I miss the ocean, the vibrant, chaotic energy, and most of all, my family and friends. I miss the easy familiarity of home.

But moving to Vienna has given me a new world. It's given me four distinct seasons, from snowy Christmas markets to lazy summer days swimming in the Alte Donau. It's given me access to centuries of art, music, and history just a tram ride away. It's given me a new language, a new perspective, and a profound appreciation for both the home I left behind and the one I'm building here.

The journey from Durban to Vienna is more than just a long-haul flight. It's a bridge between two worlds, two cultures, and two versions of myself. And while it's been challenging, it's a beautiful, life-changing adventure I wouldn't trade for anything.

Thanks for reading. — Brendon
