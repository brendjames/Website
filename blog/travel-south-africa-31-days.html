Coast to Bush: The 31-Day South Africa Road Trip I'd Plan for a Friend

By Brendon James Kirk October 6, 2026 11 min read

Ever since I moved to Vienna, I get the same message a few times a year: "We're finally doing South Africa. Where do we go?" My answer is always the same. If you can find a month, give it a month. South Africa is not a country you tick off in a week; it's a whole world squeezed into one map, and the magic is in the space between the postcards.

This is the route I'd plan for a friend. It starts with mountains and wine in Cape Town, follows the coast east through forests, lagoons and the wildly beautiful Transkei, warms up in Durban, climbs into the Drakensberg, drops into the bush at Kruger, and finishes in the city that never pretends to be anything it isn't: Joburg.

The Route at a Glance

  1. Cape Town: 5 days
  2. The Garden Route: 5 days
  3. The Wild Coast (Coffee Bay, Hole in the Wall, Mdumbi): 5 days
  4. Durban: 3 days
  5. The Drakensberg: 4 days
  6. Kruger National Park: 5 days
  7. Johannesburg: 4 days

That's 31 days on the ground. Fly into Cape Town, fly home from Johannesburg, and add a day at each end for jet lag and lost luggage. Trust me.

Before You Go

When to travel

There's no perfect month for the whole route, because you're crossing very different climates. Cape Town is best in its dry summer (roughly November to March) and gets wet and windy in winter. Kruger is the opposite: game viewing is easiest in the dry winter (May to September), when the bush thins out and animals gather at water. The Drakensberg gets afternoon thunderstorms in summer and can see snow in winter.

My pick for this route is the shoulder seasons: September to November or March to May. You get decent weather in the Cape, comfortable hiking in the Berg, warm water in Durban and good game viewing in Kruger, with fewer crowds and lower prices than the December holidays. If you're in the Cape between June and November, keep an eye out for southern right whales along the coast.

Getting around

This is a road trip, so rent a car. Public transport between these places is limited, and half the joy is the drive itself. A few things to know:

  • We drive on the left. Overtaking etiquette is real: slower cars move onto the shoulder to let you pass, and a quick flash of the hazards says thank you.
  • Don't drive after dark outside the cities. Potholes, unlit roads, pedestrians and livestock all become invisible. This matters most on the Wild Coast, where cows and goats own the road.
  • Picking up in Cape Town and dropping off in Johannesburg means a one-way fee. Ask for it upfront and compare companies; it varies a lot.
  • Petrol stations are full-service. An attendant fills up, cleans the windscreen and checks your tyres if you ask. A small tip is appreciated.
  • Short on time? Domestic flights are cheap and quick. Flying Cape Town to Durban or Durban to Joburg can save you two long driving days.

Visas, money and power

  • Many passport holders, including most of the EU, the UK and the US, can visit visa-free for up to 90 days. Check your own nationality before booking, and make sure your passport has two blank pages; border officials are strict about this.
  • The currency is the South African rand (ZAR). Cards work almost everywhere in the cities. Carry cash for the Wild Coast, markets, car guards and tips. Use ATMs inside shopping centres rather than on the street.
  • Tipping: around 10–15% at restaurants, plus a little for petrol attendants and the car guards who watch your car in parking lots.
  • Plugs are mostly the big three-pin Type M, with some Type N. Buy an adapter when you land; every supermarket sells them.
  • Power cuts ("load-shedding") have eased a lot in recent years, but they can come back. Most guesthouses are set up for it, and the EskomSePush app will tell you if a schedule is in force.
  • Grab a local SIM (Vodacom or MTN have the best coverage) at the airport. You'll need your passport to register it.

Staying safe, sensibly

South Africa has a reputation, and it's not invented. But millions of visitors have brilliant, trouble-free trips by following the same common-sense rules locals live by. Keep your phone and camera out of sight on busy streets. Never leave anything visible in a parked car. Use Uber or Bolt in the cities at night instead of walking. Ask your host which areas to avoid; they'll give you a straight answer. And don't let any of this scare you off: the warmth of the people here is the thing visitors talk about most when they get home.

Days 1–5: Cape Town

The Mother City is the most beautiful way to start any trip. I've written a whole love letter to Cape Town, so here's the short version for five days:

  • Table Mountain. Take the cableway or hike up Platteklip Gorge. Go on the first clear, calm day you get; when the "tablecloth" cloud rolls in or the wind picks up, the cableway closes. For sunrise or sunset, hike Lion's Head instead.
  • The Peninsula day. Drive Chapman's Peak, have lunch in Kalk Bay, meet the penguins at Boulders Beach in Simon's Town, and stand at Cape Point where the wind tries to take your hat.
  • Kirstenbosch and the Bo-Kaap. A morning in one of the world's great botanical gardens, an afternoon wandering the colourful streets of the Bo-Kaap with a Cape Malay curry.
  • Robben Island. Book online well ahead. Tours can be cancelled when the sea is rough, so don't leave it for your last day.
  • The Winelands. Stellenbosch or Franschhoek for a day of wine, cheese and mountain views. Take a tour or a wine tram so nobody has to drive.

Getting on: Cape Town to the Garden Route is about 5–6 hours on the N2. In whale season, detour via Hermanus for a morning of whale watching from the cliffs.

Days 6–10: The Garden Route

The Garden Route is where the Western Cape goes soft and green: lagoons, indigenous forest, and beaches that seem to go on forever. Split your nights between two bases so you're not packing every morning: two nights in Wilderness and three around Knysna or Plettenberg Bay.

  • Wilderness. Tea-coloured rivers, a hidden waterfall at the end of a forest trail, and the Map of Africa viewpoint. It's my favourite spot on the whole route; I wrote about why Wilderness will steal your soul.
  • Knysna. Look out over the Knysna Heads, walk in the Featherbed Nature Reserve, and eat fresh oysters on the lagoon.
  • Plettenberg Bay. Hike the Robberg Nature Reserve peninsula, where seals laze on the rocks below the trail.
  • Tsitsikamma. Cross the suspension bridge at Storms River Mouth. If you're brave, the Bloukrans Bridge bungee is one of the highest bridge jumps in the world.

Getting on: this is the longest drive of the trip. From Plett to Coffee Bay is roughly 10 hours, so use day 10 as a travel day and break it in two: overnight around Chintsa or East London, then do the final stretch through Mthatha in daylight.

Days 11–15: The Wild Coast

This is the part of the trip people remember forever. The Wild Coast, the old Transkei, is rolling green hills, colourful rondavels, dirt roads and cows on the beach. It's raw and remote, and it moves at the pace of the tides. I wrote a whole post about discovering the magic of the Transkei; here's how to spend five days there.

  • Coffee Bay (2 nights). The lively, bohemian gateway: backpackers, surfers and bonfires. Hire a local guide for the cliff paths and a visit to a Xhosa village.
  • Hole in the Wall. Walk the roughly 9 km coastal hike south from Coffee Bay to the famous rock arch, known in Xhosa as esiKhaleni, "the place of sound". At high tide the waves boom straight through it. Arrange a lift back so you're not racing the sunset.
  • Mdumbi (3 nights). Further north and even quieter: a river estuary, a long white beach and some excellent surf. The lodges here work closely with the local community, so your money stays in the village. Come here to switch off completely.

Practical stuff: the roads will give you a "Transkei massage", so a car with decent ground clearance helps. Signal is patchy, ATMs are few, and shops are basic. Bring cash, snacks and a headlamp, and fill your tank in Mthatha.

Getting on: Mthatha to Durban is around 5 hours via Kokstad and the South Coast.

Days 16–18: Durban

Home. Durban is warm in every sense: bathtub-warm Indian Ocean, humid air and the friendliest people in the country (I'm biased, but I'm right). For the full sales pitch, read Surf, Spice, and Zulu Soul.

  • The Golden Mile. Walk, cycle or skate the beachfront promenade. Swim only at beaches with lifeguards and shark nets.
  • Bunny chow. A hollowed-out loaf of bread filled with curry, eaten with your hands. It's Durban's gift to the world. Don't leave without one.
  • Spice and stadium. Smell your way through the Victoria Street Market, then ride the SkyCar to the top of the Moses Mabhida Stadium arch for the view.
  • Umhlanga. A sunset walk past the lighthouse on the promenade, then dinner by the sea.

Getting on: the central and northern Drakensberg are about 3–4 hours away on the N3.

Days 19–22: The Drakensberg

The uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and one look at the basalt walls explains why. Split your four days between the northern Berg and the central Berg.

  • The Amphitheatre and Tugela Falls. The Sentinel hike climbs to the top of the escarpment via a pair of chain ladders and looks down on Tugela Falls, often called the second-highest waterfall in the world. Start early: summer storms build in the afternoon.
  • Cathedral Peak or Champagne Castle. Easier valley walks, rock pools and big mountain views from a cosy lodge.
  • San rock art. The Drakensberg holds one of the largest collections of rock paintings in Africa. Visit with a guide at Kamberg or Giant's Castle to understand what you're looking at.

Pack layers. Even in summer, mornings up here can be properly cold.

Getting on: the drive to Kruger is around 7–8 hours. Make it a day of it: overnight in Graskop and drive the Panorama Route (God's Window, Bourke's Luck Potholes and the Three Rondavels above the Blyde River Canyon) on your way into the park.

Days 23–27: Kruger National Park

Kruger is nearly two million hectares of bush, bigger than some countries, and it's where South Africa stops being a holiday and starts being a story. I wrote about it in The Kruger Roar, and built an interactive map of 177,000+ wildlife sightings if you want to see where the animals actually turn up.

  • Book early. SANParks rest camps such as Skukuza, Lower Sabie and Satara fill up months ahead, especially in school holidays.
  • Stay in the south for your first visit. It has the highest density of game, and the road along the Sabie River between Skukuza and Lower Sabie is famous for good reason.
  • Mix it up. Self-drive during the day, then book a ranger-led sunrise walk and a night drive. That's the only way to see the bush after the gates close.
  • Know the rules. Gate times are strict, speed limits are low, and you never get out of your car except at marked spots. Rangers enforce this, and so do the lions.
  • Malaria: Kruger is in a malaria area. Talk to a travel clinic before you go about whether prophylaxis is right for you.

Patience is everything here. Pull over at a waterhole, turn the engine off, and wait. The bush rewards the people who stop rushing.

Getting on: it's roughly 5 hours from the southern gates to Johannesburg on the N4.

Days 28–31: Johannesburg

Joburg (Jozi, Egoli, the City of Gold) is loud, creative and unapologetically real. Many visitors skip it. Don't. You can't understand South Africa without it.

  • The Apartheid Museum. Give it at least half a day. It's heavy, essential and brilliantly done.
  • Soweto. Go with a local guide: Vilakazi Street, the Mandela House and the Hector Pieterson Memorial, then lunch at a shisa nyama.
  • Constitution Hill. A former prison that is now home to the country's highest court. It tells South Africa's story from both sides.
  • Maboneng and Braamfontein. Street art, galleries, coffee and the Saturday Neighbourgoods Market.
  • The Cradle of Humankind. About an hour out of town, where some of the world's most important human fossils were found. Visit the Sterkfontein Caves and Maropeng.

Use Uber or the Gautrain to get around, book guided tours for the inner city and Soweto, and on your last night, raise a glass to the month you just had.

What to Pack

  • Layers and a rain jacket. You'll see beach weather, mountain cold and highveld thunderstorms in the same month.
  • Proper walking shoes for Table Mountain, Robberg, Hole in the Wall and the Berg.
  • Binoculars for Kruger. They change everything.
  • Strong sunscreen, a hat and insect repellent.
  • A headlamp, a power bank and some cash for the Wild Coast.

One Last Thing

Don't try to see everything. This route is already big, and the best moments won't be on any list: a conversation with a stranger at a braai, a road you weren't planning to take, a sunset you stopped the car for. Leave room for those. South Africa is generous like that.

Go well, and if you do this trip, send me a message. I'd love to hear where it took you.

Travel times, prices, visa rules and park regulations change. Check official sources such as SANParks and your government's travel advice before you book.
